# Caves demand honest uncertainty, not tighter scan weights

For cave surveys hundreds of metres long, the evidence favours a LiDAR-inertial odometry that knows how little it knows: per-point information that is capped and noise-aware, degeneracy detected per direction and answered with a soft IMU-weighted prior, ms-level IMU-LiDAR timing, and a gated, outlier-robust loop closure followed by offline batch refinement. Independent tests show that handheld SLAM in tunnels is far less accurate than vendor figures: global errors of **decimetres over 120 m** and tens of centimetres over 750 m, accumulating mostly along the passage axis, against vendor claims of 5-30 mm and **0.03 % drift** (3 cm per 100 m). Cave-survey grading offers only coarse yardsticks (UIS expected error ratios of 2 % for Grade 5 and 1 % for Grade 6), so the acceptance criteria below are the researchers' own proposals, built from those yardsticks and from independent measurements. For degeneracy and weighting the literature is thin where dMapper needs it most: no peer-reviewed real-cave evaluation of degeneracy mitigation, no Mid-360 tunnel benchmark, no NDT-specific degeneracy study, and no published use of consistency tests (NIS/NEES) to calibrate a LiDAR term. The top-ranked recommendation, correcting the unscaled per-point NDT weight, therefore rests on inference from FAST-LIO2/VoxelMap practice and ICP-covariance studies, not on a direct precedent.

## Survey grades give loose ratios, and independent tests contradict vendor drift claims

Cave survey grades were written for compass-and-tape work and constrain instruments and station positions, not drift. BCRA Grade 5 specifies angles of +-1 deg and station positions under 10 cm; Grade 6 specifies +-0.5 deg and under 2.5 cm ([BCRA](https://bcra.org.uk/surveying)). The UIS ontology adds an expected error ratio per grade: **G3 <10 %, G4 <5 %, G5 <2 %, G6 <1 %** ([UIS ontology](https://ontology.uis-speleo.org/mappingGrades/)). That table is a version-1 listing, and the version-2 PDF could not be fetched, so the ratios are unverified against the current standard. Suffix letters are non-numeric, but B (loops closed and adjusted) and E (electronic data) are what a LiDAR survey would claim. Practice runs tighter than the grades: surveyors with compass, clinometer and tape accept roughly 2 % loop misclosure, DistoX users re-check any loop above 0.5 m regardless of length ([Survex list](https://lists.survex.com/pipermail/survex/2014-November/001842.html), secondhand), and a calibrated DistoX loop closed at 0.1 % ([texascavers](https://www.mail-archive.com/texascavers@texascavers.com/msg73412.html), forum-level). The Compass documentation cautions that percentage closure ignores instrument accuracy and that longer loops should score smaller percentages ([Compass](https://fountainware.com/compass/HTML_Help/Project_Manager/loopquality.htm)), so a single threshold is a convention, not a law.

Vendor figures are controlled-environment statements. Emesent lists Hovermap ST SLAM drift of +-0.03 % and typical underground mapping accuracy of +-15 mm ([Emesent](https://knowledge.emesent.com/articles/hovermap-specifications)); NavVis quotes 5 mm (1 sigma) in a 500 m2 test space and says absolute accuracy depends on environment size and control points ([NavVis](https://knowledge.navvis.com/docs/device-comparison-matrix)); GeoSLAM quotes up to 6 mm relative accuracy ([geo-matching](https://geo-matching.com/products/zeb-horizon)). Every vendor guide then tells users to close loops often: NavVis every 15-30 m ([NavVis tips](https://knowledge.navvis.com/docs/scanning-tips-1)), heritage guidance within 20 minutes and with start equal to end ([GIM International](https://www.gim-international.com/content/article/handheld-3d-laser-scanning-in-underground-heritage-documentation)), and Emesent recommends control points along the full length of featureless tunnels ([Emesent](https://knowledge.emesent.com/articles/scanning-roads-and-tunnels)).

Independent measurements are less flattering. In a roughly 120 m sandstone adit with a Riegl TLS reference, GeoSLAM ZEB Horizon, an actuated VLP-16 rig and a Livox Horizon rig showed global M3C2 standard deviations of **364, 281 and 232 mm**, all above 200 mm; the authors blame drift, with double walls and floors in the Livox cloud and outliers concentrated far from the loop closure, while cross-section fits stayed at 24-114 mm ([Trybala et al. 2023](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XLVIII-1-W1-2023/517/2023/isprs-archives-XLVIII-1-W1-2023-517-2023.pdf)). In a 750 m mine gallery a Hovermap ST-X was precise over short sections but accumulated error along the tunnel axis (slight compression requiring scale correction) with transverse deviations of tens of centimetres, and a double pass reduced the transverse error without removing the systematic part ([Kremen et al. 2024](https://actamont.fberg.tuke.sk/pdf/2024/n3/10kremen.pdf); read via a search summary, exact RMSE not retrieved). A test in a 120 m tunnel reports Hovermap two-way RMSD of 7 mm, but that comes from Emesent's own blog and the reference is not stated ([Emesent blog](https://www.emesent.com/blog/2025/01/23/hovermap-outperforms-tls-in-independent-testing)). The honest reading is that sub-centimetre vendor numbers describe local relative accuracy, and global drift over hundreds of metres in a weakly constrained passage is decimetres unless loops or control points remove it.

Checking a cave map without ground truth works at two levels. Local crispness can be measured without a reference by mean map entropy and mean plane variance, but those say nothing about global geometry ([MapEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.17928v2); [no-ground-truth metric](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.10402)). Global geometry needs the cave surveyor's tools: closure percentage, scanning the passage in both directions and comparing the clouds, and handheld references. The literature offers no published protocol for hand references (laser-disto distances, compass bearings, plumb lines, water level) in SLAM validation, so that part of the table below is the researchers' own suggestion. Use-specific accuracy requirements (volume, hydrology, archaeology) were not found at all; a 1 % scale error gives about 3 % volume error by simple cubic scaling (inference).

| Criterion (proposed, per 100 m of path) | Minimum | Target | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3D end-point misclosure of a closed loop, before any loop-closure correction | <=1 m (1 %) | <=0.3 m (0.3 %) | UIS G6 ratio <1 %; independent 120 m tests show 0.2-0.36 m std, so 0.3 % is competitive; DistoX practice 0.1-0.5 %. Inference |
| Same, after loop closure and batch refinement | <=0.3 m | <=0.1 m (0.1 %) | DistoX-class 0.1 %; MARBLE 0.014 % on a 2.2 km garage with loop closure ([survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2208.01787)). Inference |

A criterion is reported separately for local (relative) and global error, and always with its distance from the nearest loop closure, because both independent studies find errors growing with that distance.

## Degeneracy is normal underground, and soft, noise-aware handling is the best-supported answer

DARPA SubT teams treated degeneracy as routine. The CERBERUS winner thresholded Hessian eigenvalues, skipped ill-conditioned modules and propagated the previous estimate, added zero-velocity factors after 0.5 s of agreed stillness, and kept static LiDAR covariances ([survey of six SubT teams](https://arxiv.org/html/2208.01787); [CERBERUS](https://arxiv.org/html/2207.04914)). Team CTU-CRAS-Norlab optimised only 4 DoF with roll and pitch from the IMU, and MARBLE reached 0.4 deg pitch/roll accuracy from a good IMU so it could run on inertial data alone for extended periods ([survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2208.01787)). CSIRO tilted a VLP-16 by 45 deg to see floor and roof in narrow tunnels ([survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2208.01787)).

Detection methods agree on the structure and differ in the test. Almost all eigendecompose the Gauss-Newton information matrix, with translation and rotation blocks separate. Zhang's minimum-eigenvalue threshold needs retuning per sensor, with reported tuned values from 24 to 150 and one dataset where zero (no handling) was best ([Hatleskog and Alexis](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10784v2)). X-ICP counts, per direction, the correspondences that actually constrain it, using fixed kappa values across sensors ([X-ICP](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2211.16335)). Hatleskog and Alexis model point noise, show that noise inflates the information matrix (E[H_hat] = H + Sigma, so a clean eigenvalue test over-reports information), and replace thresholds with a per-direction probability that signal exceeds ten times the noise; in the Seemuhle mine they reached **0.04 m APE against 0.57 m for a tuned Zhang** ([Hatleskog and Alexis](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10784v2)). That is one quantitative dataset, so it is encouraging, not settled.

Handling options run from dropping the direction to a soft pull towards the prior. The field comparison in a bicycle tunnel found every active method clearly better than plain point-to-plane (ATE 1.1 m against 2.9 m, which duplicated the map); hard equality constraints were the most consistent without tuning, while inequality and Tikhonov variants were best but were tuned on that very tunnel ([Tuna et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.11809v2)). The same study warns that a wrong minimum can have a well-conditioned Hessian because conditioning degrades with residual size, that robust Cauchy kernels drifted most, and that Zhang's method produced a rotationally misaligned map that translation metrics hid. D2-LIO regularises weak directions towards the IMU-propagated guess with weight scaled by 1/lambda and by the preintegration covariance, gaining about 0.04 m on GEODE tunnels but only marginally in mild ones ([D2-LIO](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.14355v1)). Intensity (COIN-LIO), radar, thermal and learned-velocity aids exist but showed no gain or were only read at abstract level ([GEODE](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04961v2)).

The researchers' inference is that dMapper's soft, IMU-weighted structure matches the Hatleskog and D2-LIO approach, and that the IMU can only carry a degenerate direction for seconds. Their own arithmetic, assuming perfect gravity removal, gives 10 cm of position error after about 31 s from velocity random walk at 0.001 m/s2/sqrt(Hz) but after only **4.5 s from a 1 mg uncorrected bias** and about 11 s from a 0.1 deg gravity misalignment, so for passages longer than roughly 5-15 s the axial position is unobservable from the sensors and is closed only by loops or control points. No published measurement of MEMS bridging distance in a tunnel was found. Pitfalls to flag: fixed eigenvalue thresholds scale with point count and density, so any threshold must be normalised to behave the same on L2 and Mid-360S, and nothing found evaluates degeneracy detection for NDT cells or in a real cave.

## Over-confident scan weights make everything else tuning folklore

The weighting question is where the notes bear directly on dMapper. FAST-LIO2 gives every point one constant residual covariance (LASER_POINT_COV = 0.001) and adds information linearly in the point count ([FAST_LIO source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hku-mars/FAST_LIO/master/src/laserMapping.cpp); [paper](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2107.06829)); it works because the 0.5 m voxel downsampling caps the count and the constant is tuned as a global trust knob, with IMU "covariances" inflated to 0.1 to avoid being overruled ([config](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/hku-mars/FAST_LIO/master/config/mid360.yaml)). VoxelMap is the principled exception: range noise (0.04 m) and bearing noise (0.1 deg) are propagated through the point and the plane to a per-point residual variance ([VoxelMap](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.07082)). Closed-form ICP covariances ignore correlated errors and wrong correspondences; Censi-type estimates were off by orders of magnitude from sampled covariances (average KL divergence 1e6 to 1e8 against tens for the alternatives) ([CELLO-3D](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1810.01470)), and Brossard's remedy was an added shared-bias term and propagation of the initial-guess uncertainty, not a scale on the inverse information ([Brossard et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1909.05722)). Fixed covariances are also fragile: scaling FAST-LIO2's noise by 100x or 0.01x moved ATE from about 0.8 m to 45.8 m on one sequence, while an adaptive filter held 0.18-0.20 m ([AKF-LIO](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2503.06891)).

The researchers infer three things for an NDT map whose per-point weight is the cell inverse covariance with no further scale. First, the cell spread counts the rock's roughness as if it were noise at every point, while neighbouring points in one voxel-sized patch share the same error, so the effective number of independent constraints is closer to the scan area divided by the voxel area than to the point count; a 15-30x optimistic sigma corresponds to an N/N_eff of 225-900. Second, a better per-point variance is sigma_pt^2 + n^T Sigma_cell n including the cell-mean uncertainty (cell covariance over its point count), with an eigenvalue floor near the sensor noise variance (the sensor's 2-4 cm gives about (0.02-0.04 m)^2) and at least 5-10 points per cell (VoxelMap uses 5, GICP 10). Third, the LiDAR and IMU weights must be set together, since inflating the IMU 10x or more is common mainly to compensate an over-weighted LiDAR term; the user's own L2 accelerometer values (0.0013 noise density, 0.1 vibration floor) already follow that pattern. Consistency can be checked by the mean normalised innovation squared of scan matching against the motion prior, one global trust scale tuned until it is near one per degree of freedom; no LIO paper was found doing this, so it is an untested but cheap proposal. Timing is a precondition: LI-Init estimates time offset, extrinsics, gravity and bias online ([Zhu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.11006)), and by the researchers' arithmetic an 80 ms offset at 0.5 rad/s is 2.3 deg of error, so a tight gyro term is justified only once offsets are a few milliseconds. Per-point deskewing matters because 1.5 m/s at 10 Hz displaces a scan by 15 cm, an order of magnitude above point noise; this is also inference, since the deskewing literature was not retrieved.

## Loop closure underground fails by aliasing, so it is gated and its outliers are rejected

Successful DARPA systems closed loops cautiously or not at all. LAMP names repetitive structure and long featureless corridors as the cause of spurious closures and uses an automatic outlier-rejecting back-end ([LAMP](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.01744v1)); the CoSTAR finals used covariance- or distance-gated candidates, TEASER++ or SAC-IA with GICP refinement, and graduated non-convexity (GNC) that rejected up to 90 % of outlier closures ([survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2208.01787)); CSIRO gated by Mahalanobis distance instead of place recognition; MARBLE cut its search radius to 2 m to stop false matches between tunnel levels, which bears directly on shafts and multi-floor passages. CTU-CRAS-Norlab flew caves for 600 m with under 1 m error and no loop closure at all ([survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2208.01787)). Offline refinement helps modestly when the start is already good: hierarchical bundle adjustment cut ATE on Newer College from 0.35 m (FAST-LIO2) to 0.26 m, and CERBERUS' server graph reduced mean translation error from 0.23-1.29 m to 0.20-0.36 m while worsening one robot's rotation ([HBA](https://arxiv.org/html/2209.11939); [survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2208.01787)). The lesson is that batch refinement polishes a trajectory that is already roughly right, and cannot repair one with a stall. Hilti 2023 winners combined odometry, global-registration loop closure and a factor graph, reaching 3.3 cm RMSE single-session on construction and parking sites, which sets an upper bound for what structured environments allow, not a cave expectation ([Hilti 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.09765v2)).

## Benchmarks show sensor field of view and passage smoothness decide success

GEODE, the largest relevant benchmark, shows mine-style rough tunnels solvable at roughly 0.1-0.2 m ATE for 64-line sensors with FAST-LIO2, DLIO and LIO-SAM, while smooth shield tunnels defeat nearly every method (five sequences failed for all; best method RELEAD 1.43-79.6 m on those it completed) ([GEODE](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04961v2)). The Livox Avia, a small-FOV non-repetitive sensor, ranged from 0.15 m to **tens of metres** in rough tunnels and failed every method on stairs, which the authors attribute to limited FOV. Mid-360 was not tested there, and no head-to-head of FAST-LIO2, Point-LIO, iG-LIO or DLIO on a Mid-360 in a tunnel or cave was found. The researchers place the Mid-360S (360 x 59 deg) between the Avia and 64-line spinners, expect rough rock to behave like "Tunneling" more than "Shield" (about 0.1-0.3 m over tens to hundreds of metres), and read that as the case for offline refinement. All of this is inference. Mounting evidence is thin: CSIRO's 45 deg tilt is the only published support for vertical or tilted mounts, and the handheld Mid-360S guide found is a retail blog warning that handles and flexible masts spoil mapping ([openelab](https://openelab.io/pl/blogs/learn/livox-mid360s-handheld-3d-scanner-slam-mapping-guide)). For stairs and shafts, MARBLE's observation that many points in narrow passages fall below minimum range, degrading rotation, supports ignoring near-range points where they are noise, and Team Explorer's adaptive voxel size (small in corridors, large in caves) matches LOCUS 2.0's constant-point-count adaptive voxel filter ([survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2208.01787); [LOCUS 2.0](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.11784)).

## Ranked design choices for the NDT scan-to-map odometry

The ranking weighs expected gain in map precision, strength of evidence, and fit with dMapper's structure (gyro, accelerometer and bias terms, per-point cell-inverse-covariance weights). Rank 1 and 2 are the researchers' inferences from adjacent literature; none has a direct cave precedent.

| Rank | Design choice | Why, and how strong is the evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cap and correct the per-point LiDAR information: effective-sample scaling (about one constraint per voxel-sized patch) plus residual variance sigma_pt^2 + n^T Sigma_cell n, with a cell eigenvalue floor near sensor noise | Directly addresses the stated overconfidence; FAST-LIO2 and VoxelMap show the mechanism, CELLO-3D and Brossard show the optimism. Inference, no direct NDT precedent |
| 2 | Calibrate the weights with a window-level NIS test (mean near 1 per degree of freedom) and retune the IMU noise together with the LiDAR term | Cheap diagnostic, ties the two knobs together; no LIO precedent found |
| 3 | Per-direction degeneracy information test, translation and rotation separately, noise-biased (Hatleskog style), normalised to point count and sensor | Best transfer record untuned (mine APE 0.04 m vs 0.57 m); one dataset |
| 4 | Soft prior on weak directions from the IMU-propagated guess, weight from detection probability and preintegration covariance; keep the well-constrained subspace unchanged; check the final cost against prior-only | D2-LIO and Hatleskog; hard constraints were the most consistent untuned in the one field study, so keep them as a fallback |
| 5 | IMU-LiDAR time offset within a few ms and extrinsic rotation well below 1 deg before trusting the gyro; estimate or verify with LI-Init-style checks per recording | An 80 ms lag at 0.5 rad/s is 2.3 deg; arithmetic, plus LI-Init |
| 6 | Per-point deskewing from the trajectory | 15 cm in-scan motion at walking speed; inference |
| 7 | Gated loop closure: covariance or distance gate, geometric verification, GNC-style outlier rejection, 2 m-class radius across levels or shafts | LAMP, CoSTAR, MARBLE |
| 8 | Treat the IMU as a bridge of seconds: axial position for longer featureless stretches closes only by loops, control marks or a re-walk; flag such stretches in the report | Researchers' arithmetic (4.5-31 s); no measurement |
| 9 | Offline batch refinement (hierarchical or windowed bundle adjustment) after a stall-free odometry, reported with its gain | HBA 26 % ATE gain on Newer College; modest |
| 10 | Near-range point rejection when noise dominates, adaptive cell size for narrow versus large passages, minimum 5-10 points per cell | MARBLE, Team Explorer, LOCUS 2.0, VoxelMap |
| 11 | Zero-velocity factors, roll and pitch from gravity, and unreliable-normal rejection | CERBERUS, CTU-CRAS, Hatleskog; dMapper already has rest detection and an accelerometer term |

## Conclusion

The sharpest result of this synthesis is a mismatch of confidence: vendors and many LIO systems state millimetre accuracy because their scan terms are over-weighted and their noise is white, while independent tunnel tests find decimetres, so the first job for a cave map is honest uncertainty, not a cleverer matcher. That makes the correction of dMapper's per-point weight and a statistical-consistency check the highest-leverage steps, ahead of any new degeneracy machinery, and it suggests expressing acceptance as paired local and global numbers measured against distance from the nearest loop closure.

Open gaps limit how far the rankings can be trusted. No peer-reviewed real-cave quantitative evaluation of degeneracy mitigation, no Mid-360 or Mid-360S underground comparison, no NDT-specific degeneracy study, no published hand-reference protocol for SLAM validation in caves, no use-specific accuracy requirements, and no verified UIS version-2 error ratios were found; the Josef mine RMSE values, the full CERBERUS, Wildcat and LOCUS 2.0 results, the deskewing literature, and DLIO, KISS-ICP and LIO-SAM weighting details were not read. The first dMapper experiment worth running is therefore a cave or equivalent loop recording scored with the table above, once with the current weight and once with the capped, noise-aware weight, with the NIS statistic reported for both.
