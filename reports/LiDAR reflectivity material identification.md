# Turning Unitree L2 intensity into cave geology

The Unitree L2's 8-bit intensity can support a few broad material classes in caves and outcrops, roughly three to five. It cannot support fine lithology, and it gets there only after three steps that nobody has published for this sensor. First, characterise what the byte actually is. Second, run a targetless self-calibration on dMapper's own registered data that fits a range curve g(R) and an incidence-angle curve f(α). Third, average many observations per map cell. **The open SDK copies the raw `uint8` "Point Reflect [0-255]" straight into `intensity` with no compensation, and Unitree publishes no definition of the scale.** The first experiment is therefore to find out whether the firmware already compensates for range. At 905 nm the most useful physics for a geologist is that **the laser sits on the ferric-iron crystal-field band (hematite 848–906 nm, goethite 909–1022 nm)**. Iron-stained surfaces therefore look darker than their visible colour suggests, while calcite, gypsum and clean flowstone stay bright. **Wetness is the dominant confounder:** it darkens rock through optical effects rather than through water absorption, which is weak at 905 nm (about 0.07 cm⁻¹). It also makes "dark" ambiguous: wet limestone, dry shale and iron-stained sandstone can look alike. The best published cave result is >84 % accuracy for marble vs lherzolite. It used corrected TLS intensity, almost certainly from a 1550 nm-class Riegl, and **no study using a 905 nm mobile scanner for cave lithology was found**. dMapper is well placed to fill that gap. After loop closure and consistency it holds final poses, NDT-cell covariances (normals) and dense revisits of the same walls from many ranges and angles. Those are exactly the inputs a Levinson/Höfle-style per-cell joint fit needs. The catch is that today's output LAZ throws away range and the sensor position.

A note on evidence. Most papers cited here were read only as abstracts, bibliographic records or search-result extracts, because the publisher sites were blocked during research. Unitree manual statements are also search extracts. Unitree SDK code, Mandeye code and dMapper code were read directly. Statements marked *(inference)* are reasoning that no source tested. Statements marked *(unverified)* are background values that still need checking against the primary source.

## The L2 "reflectivity" byte is undefined and must be measured first

The L2 packet struct `LidarPointData` carries `uint16_t ranges[300]` (mm) and `uint8_t intensities[300]`, commented only as "Point Reflect [0-255]". The calibration parameters `range_bias`/`range_scale` correct range, and nothing corrects intensity ([unilidar_sdk2 protocol header](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/unitreerobotics/unilidar_sdk2/main/unitree_lidar_sdk/include/unitree_lidar_protocol.h)). In `parseFromPacketToPointCloud`, **`point3d.intensity = intensities[j]` is a direct copy**, and `ring` is hard-coded to 1, so the L2 exposes no per-emitter channel id ([unilidar_sdk2 utilities](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/unitreerobotics/unilidar_sdk2/main/unitree_lidar_sdk/include/unitree_lidar_utilities.h)). The L2 manual calls the field "the reflectivity of the detected object" and the sensor "3D position + 1D gray level" (search extract, [L2 User Manual](https://oss-global-cdn.unitree.com/static/Unitree%204D%20LiDAR%20L2%20User%20Manual.pdf)). The range specs are quoted at 25 °C with a 90 % target at 15 m (search extract, [Manuals+ copy](https://manuals.plus/unitree/4d-lidar-l2-from-robotics-to-infrastructure-manual)). A "Reflective Surface Performance Guide" exists, but its content could not be retrieved ([ManualsLib](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/3616985/Unitree-4d-Lidar-L2.html?page=5)). One unsourced marketing blog claims the L2 "dynamically adjusts laser power to prevent saturation" ([AliExpress wiki](https://www.aliexpress.com/s/wiki-ssr/article/lidar-4d)). If that is true, raw intensity is non-stationary and needs a model for each power state, as automotive calibration patents do with per-channel, per-power-level gain and bias ([US 11067676](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11067676)). **No paper, GitHub issue or community test of L1/L2 intensity behaviour was found.**

The comparison sensors show the range of possibilities. Livox defines 0–150 as 0–100 % Lambertian reflectance and 151–255 as retroreflective. A Livox staff member states that the value "has compensated all those factors (e.g., distance) but the incidence angle", which makes it roughly ρ·cos α (search extract, [livox-lidars group](https://groups.google.com/g/livox-lidars/c/4TphNgFpwZQ)). Livox's own Mid-360 manual warns of "a large reflectivity error" below 2 m (search extract, [Mid-360 manual](https://www.sachtleben-technology.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2024/07/LivoxMid-360UserManual.pdf)). Ouster and Velodyne both ship factory-calibrated, range-compensated reflectivity: 0–100 linear Lambertian, retroreflectors above. Neither corrects for incidence angle ([Ouster docs](https://docs.ouster.com/sensor-docs/calibrated-reflectivity), [Amerisurv on Velodyne](https://amerisurv.com/2014/03/19/velodyne-lidar-technology-achieves-calibrated-reflectivies-capability-enables-significant-cost-savings/)). The L2 could be any of three cases: a raw return strength, a Livox-style range-compensated value, or something with automatic gain control (AGC). Stacking a 1/R² correction on top of an already compensated value is the classic double-correction error.

The path from sensor to dMapper keeps the byte intact as far as the output. dMapper reads LAS `intensity` into `Point3Di::intensity` (`src/infrastructure/recording_folder_source.cpp` l.206). It writes `unsigned short` intensity unchanged into the chunk and merged LAZ through `save_result` (`src/application/lidar_odometry.cpp` l.551–570, `src/infrastructure/folder_result_sink.cpp` l.60). How dLidar writes the field was not available for inspection.

**Five cheap tests settle what the byte means** *(test designs are inferences, built on the mechanisms in the cited literature).* (1) Histogram a whole recording. A maximum of 255 with a pile-up at 255 means saturation, and a ceiling below 255 or a comb pattern means an internal non-linear mapping. (2) Walk slowly toward one flat, dry, matte wall at near-normal incidence from 15 m down to 0.1 m and plot median intensity against range. A flat curve means on-board range compensation. A decay of about R⁻² means raw power. A rise to a peak and then a fall below about 1 m means near-field optics: the biaxial overlap function, a brightness reducer or detector saturation. Kaasalainen et al. found a brightness reducer acting below about 1 m in one TLS and call knowing about such reducers and log amplifiers "of cardinal importance" ([TUNI record](https://researchportal.tuni.fi/en/publications/analysis-of-incidence-angle-and-distance-effects-on-terrestrial-l/)). (3) Repeat at one range while rotating the sensor from 0° to 80° incidence, which gives f(α). (4) Put a black and a white target side by side at the same range and check that the ratio stays constant along the walk. A ratio that changes means AGC or power switching. (5) Log intensity against time and temperature during a 30-minute static warm-up. Because `ring` = 1, a per-beam Levinson curve is impossible. The point direction in the sensor frame (azimuth/elevation of the non-repetitive pattern) is the only available proxy for a channel *(inference)*.

## Correction physics reduces to one empirical product model

For an extended Lambertian target that fills the footprint, the LiDAR range equation P_r = P_t·D_r²·η_sys·η_atm·σ/(4πR⁴β_t²), with σ = πρR²β_t²cos α, simplifies to **P_r ∝ ρ·cos α·η_atm/R²** (standard form used by Höfle & Pfeifer 2007 and Kashani et al. 2015; symbols not verified against the full texts) ([Höfle & Pfeifer record](https://heibib.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/search/Record/1885638124), [Kashani et al. 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4701271/)). The textbook correction to a reference range and normal incidence is I_c = I_raw·(R/R_ref)²/cos α. Targets smaller than the footprint, such as edges, straws and thin stalactites, follow 1/R⁴ (point) or 1/R³ (linear), so they must not enter a calibration. Real instruments do not obey 1/R² at short range. Fang et al. model a range-dependent "laser transmission function" ([visionbib listing](https://visionbib.com/bibliography/active672li1.html)). Tan & Cheng write I = f₁(ρ)·f₂(cos α)·f₃(R) as polynomials fitted by least squares ([SPIE](https://biomedicaloptics.spiedigitallibrary.org/journals/journal-of-applied-remote-sensing/volume-9/issue-01/094094/Intensity-data-correction-based-on-incidence-angle-and-distance-for/10.1117/1.JRS.9.094094.full)). Xu et al. use piecewise near/far range fits plus Oren–Nayar and reach **<3 % of spectrometer reflectance on a Riegl VZ-400i** ([Remote Sens. 9:1090](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/9/11/1090)). Krooks et al. showed that **range and incidence effects do not interact**, so the separable form f(R)·g(α) is justified ([ISPRS Annals 2013](https://isprs-annals.copernicus.org/articles/II-5-W2/145/2013/isprsannals-II-5-W2-145-2013.pdf)).

The model for the L2 is therefore I_raw = G[ η(R)·ρ·h(α)·η_atm / R² ] *(synthesis; inference)*. G is the unknown internal mapping, perhaps a log amplifier or AGC. η(R) is the near-field factor, which rises from 0 to 1 over the first metre or so in a biaxial design. One empirical g(R) = η(R)/R² is fitted in log space, as a spline that is dense below 2 m. This matters more in caves than anywhere else, because many returns lie between 0.15 m (dMapper's `min_distance_lidar`) and 5 m, which is exactly where 1/R² is "badly wrong".

For h(α), Lambert's cos α is the default, and Tan et al. report it adequate for TLS ([PolyU](https://research.polyu.edu.hk/en/publications/intensity-data-correction-for-the-distance-effect-in-terrestrial-/)). Rough rock deviates, though. Carrea et al. corrected ILRIS intensity with a simplified Oren–Nayar model for lithological differentiation, arguing that the cosine law is insufficient for rough rock ([EGU2015-10643](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU2015/EGU2015-10643.pdf?pdf)). In the monostatic LiDAR geometry, Oren–Nayar reduces to h(α) = cos α·[A + B·sin α·tan α], with A = 1 − 0.5σ²/(σ²+0.33) and B = 0.45σ²/(σ²+0.09), where σ is the facet-slope standard deviation (standard form, *unverified* against Carrea's simplified variant). It falls off more slowly than cos α. Wet flowstone and ice add a specular lobe, roughly k_s·cos^m(2α), which produces a bright glint near normal incidence and a loss elsewhere. Dark porous media behave closer to Lommel–Seeliger, which gives an almost flat response in the monostatic case *(inference; no TLS paper found)*. The ETH group's 2025 in-situ MLS calibration fits "global" and "per-segment" incidence functions from the data with little overlap. It is the closest published analogue to a handheld cave scanner ([Laasch, Medic & Wieser 2025](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XLVIII-G-2025/845/2025/)).

**Normal errors limit the angle correction.** The relative error is tan α·δα, so a 5° normal error costs about 4 % at 45°, 15 % at 60° and 50 % at 80° (computed, not cited). One automotive paper measured a 0.44 rad MSE for incidence angles derived from single-scan normals ([arXiv 2401.01439](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.01439v1)). Two consequences follow. Normals should come from the final multi-pass map, not from a single frame. And observations above roughly 60–70° should be dropped or weighted by cos α. A snow study even recommends 40° because of overcorrection ([Remote Sensing 2020](https://doaj.org/article/f0b92d0514a6432eaad3db7fd9351417)).

**Clear cave air is negligible:** a two-way loss of about 1 % over 30 m *(inference from the Kim visibility model)*. **Mist is not.** Using γ = (3.91/V)·(λ/550)^(−q) with q = 0 below 0.5 km visibility ([Radioengineering 2010](https://www.radioeng.cz/fulltexts/2010/10_02_223_227.pdf)), 1 km visibility already gives an 8–20 % range-dependent bias at 10–30 m *(computed)*. Nováková et al. explicitly correct for cave aerosol, which "similar to fog, considerably attenuates" the beam ([UPJŠ](https://www.upjs.sk/prirodovedecka-fakulta/en/actuality/laser-scanning-the-key-to-detecting-darkroom-surfaces/)). Condensation on the window shows up as a gain step that is the same at all ranges and lasts from frame to frame. A time-varying gain term handles it better than an atmospheric model does *(inference)*.

**Edges must be removed before calibrating.** Where the footprint straddles two surfaces, both range and intensity blend, giving the classic mixed pixel ([arXiv 2503.07472](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.07472)). One TLS study calls the edge effect one of the most serious intensity problems ([NTU thesis](https://tdr.lib.ntu.edu.tw/handle/123456789/68228?mode=full)). In speleothem-rich passages a sizeable share of points is likely to be edge-affected *(inference)*.

## Ferric iron, not carbonate, gives 905 nm its geological contrast

**Diagnostic carbonate features lie at 2320–2350 nm**, so a single 905 nm channel cannot separate limestone from dolomite, nor calcite from gypsum ([Kurz et al. 2012](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XXXIX-B5/417/2012/isprsarchives-XXXIX-B5-417-2012.pdf)). The wavelength coincides instead with the Fe³⁺ ⁶A₁→⁴T₁ crystal-field band. In 176 samples this band lies at **848–906 nm in hematite and 909–1022 nm in goethite** ([Scheinost et al. 1998](https://www.doi.org/10.1346/CCMN.1998.0460506)). Band centres are about 0.86 µm for hematite, 0.91 µm for goethite, 0.92 µm for maghemite and 0.98 µm for lepidocrocite ([Sherman & Waite 1985](https://msaweb.org/AmMin/AM70/AM70_1262.pdf)). A 905 nm LiDAR therefore responds unusually well to iron staining, terra rossa, ochre and red clay. "Dark at 905 nm" does not mean "dark in the visible" or "wet". Anthracite is nearly flat and very dark across the VNIR, and lower-rank coals are brighter with more features ([Spectrosc. Spectral Anal. 2019](https://m.researching.cn/articles/OJ73a3f23ddbd919e4)). **No library reflectance value at 905 nm could be read from USGS splib07, ECOSTRESS or RELAB** (their servers were blocked). The ranges in the table below are *unverified* background values to be checked against splib07a ASCII spectra before use.

Water's role differs from the 1550 nm literature most geologists know. The absorption coefficient of liquid water is **about 0.068 cm⁻¹ at 900 nm against 0.45 cm⁻¹ at the 970 nm band** ([Hale & Querry table](https://users.physics.ox.ac.uk/~pierrehumbert/PrinciplesPlanetaryClimate/Data/WorkbookDatasets/Chapter4Data/AbsorptionData/WaterAbsorptionOrig.txt)), so a mm-thick film absorbs almost nothing. Wet darkening at 905 nm comes from the reduced refractive-index contrast in pores and from the specular redirection of energy *(inference)*. VNIR reflectance falls exponentially with moisture and **saturates at low moisture**, around 20 % volumetric in the visible, while the SWIR keeps responding up to about 50 % ([Lobell & Asner 2002](https://oa.mg/work/10.2136/sssaj2002.7220), [Hick et al. 2026](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/242589/)). Earlier shorter-wavelength TLS work found reflectance insensitive to moisture above about 5 % ([EGU2014-2797](https://opac.geologie.ac.at/ais312/dokumente/EGU/EGU2014-2797.pdf)). **At 905 nm, intensity is a wet/dry flag, not a moisture gauge.** Moisture lowered brick reflectance by 30–50 % ([PMC9402562](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9402562/)). In the Gouffre Georges cave, wet surfaces biased intensity low and complicated discrimination ([Nováková et al. 2022](https://hal-ifp.archives-ouvertes.fr/GR-3T/insu-03752599v1)). Moisture and weathering also reduced the sandstone/shale contrast in outcrop ([Burton et al. 2011](https://archives.datapages.com/data/sepm/journals/081/081005/339_gsjsedres810339.htm)). Wet snow lost 18 % of its reflectance near 960 nm ([Annals of Glaciology](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/annals-of-glaciology/article/spectral-snowreflectance-models-for-grainsize-and-liquidwater-fraction-in-melting-snow-for-the-solarreflected-spectrum/AF7B8358496D46F1F3A1C0A98962B0BB)).

Most published geology successes used 1535/1550 nm scanners. Franceschi et al.'s limestone/marl result (reflectance vs clay content, **r = −0.85**, ILRIS at 1535 nm) is attributed partly to water-bearing clays absorbing at that wavelength ([Franceschi et al. 2009](https://www.earth-prints.org/entities/publication/bb9c4bdf-3743-4fbd-a5b6-db80cfd4cd4d)). At 905 nm that mechanism is much weaker, so the contrast should not be assumed to transfer quantitatively *(inference)*. The broad-band contrasts do transfer: bright carbonate against dark Fe-Mg silicate, and coal against light gangue *(inference)*. Ice-cave practice with 1550 nm Riegls separates ice from rock by intensity ([xyHt](https://xyht.com/lidarimaging/lidar-and-ice)), and a public classified dataset exists ([PANGAEA 945655](https://doi.pangaea.de/10.1594/PANGAEA.945655)). At 905 nm, clear ice absorbs less, so subsurface volume scattering and range bias grow and the 1550 nm separability will differ *(inference)*.

Under a separability rule of thumb (mean difference > 2–3× the combined σ), a realistic **10–15 % relative residual** after correction leaves about **3–5 robust classes** from intensity alone *(inference built on the published 3–10 % residuals)*. A plausible set: bright carbonate/speleothem; mid-grey bedrock; dark clastic sediment, shale or iron-stained material; very dark coal or mafic rock; wet/specular. The residual figures come from three sources: <3 % (Xu 2017), about 10 % (Höfle & Pfeifer, [dissertation](https://archiv.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/volltextserver/36930/1/hoefle_dissertation_2007.pdf)), and a CV reduced by 52 % with natural homogeneous targets only (Tan & Cheng 2020, [ECNU](https://pure.ecnu.edu.cn/en/publications/distance-effect-correction-on-tls-intensity-data-using-naturally-/)).

## Self-calibration from dMapper's own map replaces targets

dMapper can calibrate without targets because a cave survey sees each wall many times, from many ranges and angles. Levinson & Thrun calibrate per-beam remittance from a moving platform. Their method assumes that points lie on contiguous surfaces seen repeatedly and solves for the response curves with an EM-like scheme that needs accurate localisation (search extract, [Levinson thesis](https://purl.stanford.edu/zx701jr9713), [CMU RISS poster](https://riss.ri.cmu.edu/education/colby-poster.pdf)). Höfle & Pfeifer's data-driven variant fits a global correction by least squares over homogeneous areas ([record](https://heibib.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/search/Record/1885638124)). Tan & Cheng estimate the distance function from naturally homogeneous targets after removing the angle effect ([ECNU](https://pure.ecnu.edu.cn/en/publications/distance-effect-correction-on-tls-intensity-data-using-naturally-/)). Laasch et al. do the equivalent for MLS ([ISPRS 2025](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XLVIII-G-2025/845/2025/)).

Translated to dMapper *(proposed design; inference)*, each observation i of map cell p satisfies

log I_i = log g(R_i) + log h(α_i; σ_p) + log c(φ_i) + log k(t_i) + log ρ_p + ε_i.

Here g is a monotone-penalised spline in R with dense knots below 2 m. h is either cos α or Oren–Nayar with a roughness σ_p fitted per cell or per segment. c(φ) is an optional gain over scan direction in the sensor frame, standing in for the missing ring id. k(t) is a slow time/temperature or window-fogging gain per window. ρ_p is the cell's pseudo-reflectance. The problem is solved by alternating least squares with a Huber loss: fix the curves and solve each ρ_p in closed form, then fix the ρ and refit the curves. The scale ambiguity is fixed by the median ρ, or by one known panel to reach absolute (Kashani Level 3) rather than consistent (Level 2) reflectance ([Kashani et al.](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4701271/)). Only well-conditioned cells enter the fit: planar, with low scattering, an angular spread above about 20°, a range spread above 2×, n ≥ 20, and away from depth edges. Saturated 255 values, α > 70°, R below the near-field validity limit, and second-echo or mixed-pixel points are excluded.

A cell's angular response is itself informative. A fitted cos^n exponent or Oren–Nayar σ tells smooth glossy flowstone and ice (specular-leaning) from granular sediment (flatter than Lambert) *(inference)*. It is the closest thing a single-wavelength sensor has to a second spectral channel. Castorena's gradient-domain fusion is an alternative if scanners or sessions disagree ([arXiv 1611.09203](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.09203)).

**Where it fits in dMapper.** It belongs after `post_process`, once loop closure and consistency have fixed the final trajectory, and before or inside `save`. At that point every window's poses are final. Each point's raw sensor-frame coordinates are in the `PointStore`, which gives R and the beam direction directly. The world-frame point plus the cell normal gives α. This step adds two things the pipeline lacks today *(inference from the code)*.

First, a **separate fine radiometric grid** of roughly 5–10 cm surface cells. The odometry's NDT buckets are sized for registration, and their covariance comes from one map build. A dedicated grid built from the final cloud gives multi-pass normals and roughness (λ₃, plane residual) at the right scale.

Second, **output fields that preserve the geometry.** `save_result` currently writes only world xyz, intensity and timestamp, so range, incidence angle and sensor position are lost and nothing downstream can redo the correction. The raw intensity should stay in `intensity`. Corrected ρ (for example ×100 in uint16), R, α, cell ρ_p, cell std, n_obs and an edge/saturation flag should go into LAS extra bytes. A per-cell summary file should be written as well. The fitted curves g, h, c and k belong in the results JSON so they can be compared between surveys.

The computation is linear in the number of points and needs a few passes, which is cheap next to the odometry. Per CLAUDE.md's priorities, robustness wins over speed in any case. The step would change no pose, so the golden baselines are unaffected as long as the step is off by default under a new `[radiometry]` TOML section.

## Classification stays shallow: GMM first, random forest on fused features second

The literature favours handcrafted multi-scale features with a random forest. The standard eigenvalue features are linearity, planarity, scattering, omnivariance, eigenentropy, surface variation and verticality. Brodu & Lague's CANUPO computes them at several radii ([arXiv 1107.0550](https://arxiv.org/abs/1107.0550)), Weinmann et al. select scales by eigenentropy ([TUM](https://portal.fis.tum.de/en/publications/semantic-point-cloud-interpretation-based-on-optimal-neighborhood/)), and 3DMASC adds statistics of attributes such as intensity over the same neighbourhoods. With **fewer than 2,000 samples per class**, 3DMASC reaches **91–98 %** ([arXiv 2401.09481](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.09481)). Fusion pays off clearly. Spectral plus geometric features lifted outcrop lithology macro-F1 **from 0.65 to 0.82**, and stratigraphic post-constraints reached 93 % OA, ahead of SVM, XGBoost and PointNet ([Remote Sens. 17:2434](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/remotesensing/remotesensing-17-02434/article_deploy/remotesensing-17-02434.pdf)). Rockfall-slope RF scored F 95/92/85/90 % for bedrock/vegetation/talus/snow ([EGU2019-3424](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU2019/EGU2019-3424.pdf)). Coal-gangue recognition combined intensity and range into pseudo-colour images and reached 94.56 % with a DenseNet ([LOP 2024](https://www.researching.cn/ArticlePdf/m00002/2024/61/4/0437005.pdf)). For caves, Nováková's group has moved to deep-learning segmentation of floor surfaces (sediment, flowstone, bedrock) from **geometry alone** ([EGU26-807](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-807.html)), a reminder that morphology carries much of the signal. No intensity-input ablation exists for KPConv or RandLA-Net, and no GMM clustering of corrected TLS intensity for rock types was found.

The practical workflow follows from this *(inference)*. Start with a histogram or GMM of the per-cell ρ_p for each survey to see whether modes exist at all. Then train an RF on features at 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5 and 1 m: ρ statistics (median, std, P10/P90), the angular-response exponent, eigen features and small-scale roughness, plus floor/wall/ceiling position. Use about 1,000–2,000 labelled cells per class from several passages. Labels come from co-registered photos and hand samples. Validate with **spatially blocked cross-validation that holds out whole passages**, and report accuracy per range and incidence bin to expose leftover correction errors. Finish with region smoothing on the wall surface. Wetness should be recorded per survey, and thresholds should never be carried across surveys in different conditions.

The tools exist off the shelf. CloudCompare offers CANUPO and q3DMASC, the latter with CLI options `3DMASC_CLASSIFY` and `ONLY_FEATURES`. It trains with OpenCV's RF, which is slow, and its developers suggest training with scikit-learn instead ([lidar-platform docs](https://lidar-platform.readthedocs.io/en/latest/3dmasc.html), [CC forum](https://cloudcompare.org/forum/viewtopic.php?p=24779)). Feature extraction is available through PDAL `filters.covariancefeatures` ([PDAL](https://PDAL.io/stages/filters.covariancefeatures.html)), jakteristics, and pgeof, whose C++ core uses Eigen and nanoflann ([pgeof](https://pypi.org/project/pgeof/)). No open-source cave intensity-correction tool was found. Training belongs in Python. Applying the model inside dMapper is a few hundred lines of exported-tree evaluator and would need no new dependency.

## Conclusion

The project's novelty is that it can produce knowledge that does not exist yet. Three gaps stand out: a measured definition of the L2 intensity, a near-field curve for a low-cost 905 nm robotic sensor, and any 905 nm cave lithology result. dMapper's mapping-first design is the asset that makes this tractable. Once poses are final, the many revisits of each wall turn the calibration problem from "buy Spectralon and a lab" into a well-posed least-squares fit on data the survey already contains.

Geologically, the iron band is the reason to be interested rather than the obstacle. A 905 nm "darkness" map after correction is partly an Fe³⁺ map, but only where a wetness mask (dry surfaces, angular-response gloss, repeat-survey differences) has removed the stronger water effect. The realistic deliverable is a robust wet/dry map, plus bright speleothem vs bedrock vs dark clastic or iron-stained classes. It will not be limestone vs dolomite, which needs SWIR. The first change to make in dMapper costs nothing in precision: keep range, incidence angle and sensor position for every output point.
